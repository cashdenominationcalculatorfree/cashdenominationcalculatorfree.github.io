2. Step-by-Step: How to Balance a Cash Drawer (Till Reconciliation)

Reconciling a cash till at the end of each shift or business day is fundamental to internal loss prevention. Follow this standardized commercial protocol:

  1. Secure the Environment: Always pull the register till away from customer sightlines into a private back office or counting area covered by CCTV.
  2. Separate the Starting Bank (Opening Float): Every register begins its shift with an established cash float (commonly $150.00, $200.00, or $300.00). Count out this baseline amount first and place it into a safe or till drawer to prepare for the incoming shift.
  3. Count Remaining Cash by Denomination: Group and tally every bill and coin remaining in the drawer. Start from the highest denomination ($100 bills) down to loose pennies. Input these exact numbers into the calculator above.
  4. Account for Non-Cash Items: Total any paper coupons, signed customer charge slips, gift certificate stubs, or manual payment receipts.
  5. Pull the POS Summary Reading: Print the Z-Report (End-of-Day register tape) from your POS terminal (Square, Toast, Clover, Lightspeed, Shopify POS, etc.) and note the "Net Cash Sales".
  6. Calculate Variance (Over / Short): Input your Opening Float and POS Expected Sales into the Till Balancing module. The tool will calculate whether your till is balanced or has an overage or shortage.
  7. Sign and File the Count Sheet: Click Print Till Count Sheet, have both the cashier and shift manager sign the slip, and attach the POS register tape for bookkeeping.

5. Cash Over & Short Formulas and Auditing Math

In retail bookkeeping, the Cash Over / Short account is an income statement line item that records variances between physical counted funds and expected electronic transaction tapes.

The General Ledger Mathematical Formula:

Variance = Actual Counted Cash - (Starting Float + Net Cash Sales)

Interpreting the Variance Results:

  • Exact Balanced ($0.00 Variance): Every physical cent collected during the shift matches your POS transactional records.
  • Cash Over (Positive Variance): More cash is present in the drawer than recorded on POS tapes. Common causes include unrecorded sales, failing to give a customer full change, or inaccurate starting float counts.
  • Cash Short (Negative Variance): Less physical cash is in the drawer than indicated by sales receipts. Primary root causes include giving excessive change to customers, failure to record paid-outs or vendor drop receipts, skimming, or employee theft.

Acceptable Variance Thresholds

Most commercial retailers enforce a variance tolerance policy of ±$2.00 to ±$3.00 per shift. Any variance exceeding $5.00 triggers a mandatory recount by a supervisor, while variances above $20.00 typically require reviewing surveillance video and transaction audit trails.

6. Cash Weight, Dimensions & Transport Logistics

When transporting cash deposits to bank night-drops or depot vaults, understanding currency weight and physical bulk is essential for courier pricing, vehicle security, and safety planning.

Key US Currency Weight Benchmarks:

  • Weight of 1 US Banknote: Exactly 1.000 gram (0.03527 oz), composed of a 75% cotton and 25% linen blend.
  • 1,000 Banknotes (One Brick/Bundle): 1,000 grams (1 kilogram or approximately 2.2046 pounds).
  • $1,000,000 in $100 Bills: 10,000 bills = 10 kilograms (approx. 22.04 pounds). Easily fits inside a standard briefcase.
  • $1,000,000 in $20 Bills: 50,000 bills = 50 kilograms (approx. 110.23 pounds). Requires multiple secure transport crates.
  • $100 in Pennies: 10,000 pennies = 25 kilograms (approx. 55.1 pounds). Extremely heavy for retail staff to handle safely without a utility cart.

7. Cash Control Best Practices to Eliminate Shrink and Theft

Loss prevention specialists recommend five essential rules to protect retail cash flow:

  • Sole Drawer Accountability: Never allow two cashiers to share a single till simultaneously. If Cashier A takes a lunch break, assign a fresh till to Cashier B to preserve legal chain of custody.
  • Execute Mid-Day "Cash Drops": When a register accumulates more than $1,000 in large bills ($50s and $100s), transfer them to a drop-safe to reduce robbery exposure.
  • Perform Blind Till Audits: Cashiers should count their drawers without knowing the POS target sales figure beforehand. This prevents cashiers from adjusting counts to match expected totals.
  • Inspect High-Denomination Notes: Train cashiers to check security threads, color-shifting ink, and watermarks on $20, $50, and $100 bills.
  • Always Lock Tills When Stepping Away: Even a brief 30-second absence requires locking the register screen and drawer key.

What is the fastest way to count a cash drawer?
The fastest standard method is counting from highest denomination down to lowest denomination. Group bills in stacks of 25 or 50 facing the same direction. For loose coins, utilize calibrated coin cups with a precision digital money scale or pre-slotted till coin trays rather than counting individual pennies by hand.
What is a cash float (opening bank)?
A cash float (or starting bank) is the fixed sum of money placed inside a register before opening to provide change for morning transactions. Common floats range between $150 and $300 consisting primarily of $1, $5, and $10 bills along with rolls of pennies, nickels, dimes, and quarters.

What should a cashier do if a drawer is significantly short?

If the shortage persists, notify the shift supervisor immediately and document the exact discrepancy on the print audit slip.
